**Explanations to check (English):** Egyptian Arabic is not Modern Standard Arabic with a few new words: it has its own sound system. In this lesson you learn seven real words, and each one shows a pronunciation feature. Listen to the word, then compare it with the MSA form given in the Arabic meaning. In this course the pronunciation is written in Latin letters: ʾ is the glottal stop, ʿ is the letter ayn, ḥ is the strong h, and a line over a vowel marks a long vowel. **Three sound rules:** First: jim is said "g" (gamīl) and qaf often becomes a glottal stop (ʾahwa), though formal words such as the Quran often keep the real q. Second: th and dh usually become t and d (talāta, da), while in some formal words they become s and z. Third: "ay" becomes ē (bēt) and "aw" becomes ō (yōm). **Culture:** The Cairo dialect is widely understood across the Arab world thanks to Egyptian films, TV series and songs, which is why many learners start with it. Many Egyptians simply call their dialect "Masri". People are usually delighted when a foreigner tries to speak it, and they will encourage you even if you make mistakes.

**Explanations to check (English):** Egyptians often begin a conversation by greeting and asking how you are. This lesson gives you eight phrases you need every day: greeting, asking how someone is, answering, thanking, asking politely, and saying goodbye. **Masculine and feminine in greetings:** To a man we say izzayyak and to a woman izzayyik; likewise ʿāmil ēh (to a man) and ʿamla ēh (to a woman). A man answers kwayyis and a woman kwayyisa. The word ēh means "what". **Culture:** "ṣabāḥ il-khēr" is answered with "ṣabāḥ in-nūr", and "as-salāmu ʿalaykum" with "wa ʿalaykum is-salām". Egyptians use "il-ḥamdu lillāh" and "in shāʾ allāh" very often in everyday speech, and both are warm, familiar phrases. A friendly greeting and a smile are part of good manners.

**Explanations to check (English):** After the greeting comes the introduction. Egyptian Arabic has no verb "to be" in the present: you simply say "ana min maṣr". When you say your country you will often hear a word of welcome. People also commonly use respectful titles such as "yā ustāz", "yā duktōr" and "yā fandim".

**Explanations to check (English):** Family matters a great deal in Egyptian life, and people often ask about yours. This lesson gives you eight phrases to talk about your relatives. The word changes a little: ab becomes abūya and akh becomes akhūya. **Culture:** Family ties are often strong, and families often get together for a meal, especially on Fridays. Kinship words are also used in friendly address. Asking about someone's family is a sign of interest and good manners.

**Explanations to check (English):** This lesson has no new grammar; it gathers what you learned: the b- present, the past, the ha- future, negation, commands and ʿāyiz/mumkin plus a verb. You also add small discourse words that make you sound more like an Egyptian. **Review: choosing the right tense:** Use the b- present for habits and now (bāshtaghal), the past for what finished (ruḥt), and ha- for the future (haruḥ). Negate with mish or ma-...-sh, use the imperative to ask (taʿāla), and after ʿāyiz and mumkin use a verb without b-. Link events with awwil ḥāga, baʿdēn and fil-ākhir. **Culture:** Words such as māshi, ṭayyib, khalāṣ and yalla fill everyday Cairo speech and make dialogue natural and friendly. A meeting often ends with "bis-salāma" and a promise to call or an invitation soon.
