**Explanations to check (English):** Standard Chinese (普通话 pǔtōnghuà) is a tonal language: the pitch of the voice is part of the word. Pinyin is a way of writing the pronunciation with Latin letters, and a mark above the vowel shows the tone. In this lesson we use the famous mā, má, mǎ and mà and a few other real words so you can hear the differences. Listen first, then repeat out loud. **The four tones and the neutral tone:** Tone 1 (¯) is high and level: mā. Tone 2 (´) rises like a question: má. Tone 3 (ˇ) dips and then rises: mǎ. Tone 4 (`) falls sharply like a short command: mà. There is also a neutral tone with no mark, as in the second syllable of māma. The sounds zh, ch and sh are made with the tongue curled back, q and x are softer and made near the front of the palate, and ü (as in nǚ) is like saying "ee" with rounded lips. **Culture:** Mandarin is the common language that people across China use to understand each other, alongside many regional varieties of speech such as Cantonese and Shanghainese. People may speak with different accents, so do not be surprised by variety. Pinyin is also used on some signs and for typing Chinese on a phone.

**Explanations to check (English):** Basic polite phrases open the door to conversation. Greetings in Chinese are short and simple, and the phrases in this lesson work everywhere: on the street, in shops and in restaurants. Say each phrase with its correct tones, because they are part of the words. **你 and 您, and replying to thanks:** Chinese verbs do not change, only the polite word does: 你好 for ordinary use and 您好 for respect. 谢谢 is answered with 不客气 ("no need to be polite"). Note that 不 is actually pronounced bú before a fourth tone (bú kèqi), but in this course it is written with its basic tone, bù. 对不起 means "sorry" and the usual reply is 没关系 ("it doesn't matter"). **Culture:** 您 (nín) shows respect and is used with elders, teachers and people you want to treat politely, while 你 is for friends and peers. Between close friends and family, thanks are said less often because closeness makes them unnecessary. With strangers and in service situations, politeness is appreciated.

A nice thing to say at the start is 认识你很高兴 ("nice to meet you"). **Culture:** In China the family name comes first and the given name second: in 李明 (Lǐ Míng) the family name is 李 and the given name is 明. In business situations cards are often offered with both hands as a sign of respect. You can address someone with a title, such as 王老师 (Teacher Wang).

When reading phone or room numbers, 一 is often said as yāo so that it is not confused with 七.

Dishes are usually shared among everyone at the table and eaten with chopsticks. Food varies greatly from region to region: rice is more common in the south, while noodles and wheat foods are more common in the north.

Wrong replies offered: 我叫李明。 / 你好，不客气。

**Explanations to check (English):** In markets and small shops we ask the price and buy with a few words. This lesson introduces the everyday word for the currency, 块 (yuan), and the measure words 个, 杯 and 瓶, which sit between a number and a noun and are one of the most distinctive features of Chinese. **Measure words: number + measure word + noun:** In Chinese we do not say "three apples" directly but "three-pieces apple": 三个苹果. The word 个 (gè) is the most general and suits many things, 杯 (bēi) is for cups and glasses, and 瓶 (píng) for bottles. For the number two before a measure word we say 两: 两杯茶. To say "too expensive" we use 太…了: 太贵了. 一点 means "a little": 便宜一点 (a bit cheaper). **Culture:** In small markets bargaining may be accepted, but in large shops and supermarkets prices are fixed. The official currency is 人民币 (rénmínbì), and the yuan is written 元 formally but called 块 in everyday speech. Mobile payment is very common in many cities, but it is still wise to carry some cash.
