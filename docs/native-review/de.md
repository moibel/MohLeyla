These lessons are a **beta**: written carefully and checked by scripts and a second linguistic pass, but not yet by a native speaker.

**How to review.** For each row: is the target natural and correct? does the pronunciation guide match? do the Arabic, English and Indonesian meanings match? Tick the box when it is right; write the correction in the last column when it is not. Send the sheet back and the lessons are fixed and the Beta label can be removed.

## Please check these first (the reviewers were least sure)

**Pronunciation column** (capitals mark the stressed syllable; ç = the "ich" sound, kh = the "ach" sound, ew = ü, ur = ö): Kaffee (KAF-fay), Entschuldigung, brauche, Tschüss, zwanzig/fünfzig/dreißig (-tsiç), sieben, eins, Restaurant (res-to-RAHNG), München, Hamburg (final k sound), Informatik, Fahrrad, blau/grau (BLAOW/GRAOW), Sofa.

**Grammar and wording**
- `Mir tut der Bauch weh` (body part with the article, not `mein`).
- `zum Frühstück`, `zum Mittagessen` introduced as set phrases before the dative.
- `Kann ich mit Karte zahlen?` and `Ich möchte einen Kaffee` used as ready phrases early.
- `Wie sieht dein Tag aus?` in lesson 12 (aussehen early).
- `Doch` as the answer to a negative question (lesson 20 fill-in-the-blank: right answer Doch, wrong options Nein / Gern).
- `Am liebsten mache ich Sport`, `Ich höre lieber Musik`.
- `Wir werden euch aus Berlin anrufen` (anrufen takes the accusative), `seit zwei Jahren`, `Vor einem Jahr`.
- Ihr-imperatives: `Trinkt viel Tee und schlaft gut!`, `Kinder, bleibt bitte im Bett!`
- Konjunktiv II politeness: `Ich hätte gern ...`, `Könnten Sie ...`.
- `die Cola` (the item uses `die`; some say `das`).
- Culture notes: card payment habits, returns and exchanges in shops, emergency numbers 112 and 110.

**Arabic and Indonesian explanations** (the columns beside each German line) should also be read by a native speaker of those languages, especially the descriptions of the ü, ö, ä and ch sounds.

**Explanations to check (English):** German uses the Latin alphabet, and once you know a few rules most words are said the way they are written. It also has new sounds such as ü, ö and ch. In this lesson eight real words show these sounds. Listen first, then repeat out loud. **Special sounds and capital letters:** For ü, say ee with your lips rounded (in these lessons: ew). For ö, say eh with rounded lips (written ur). ä sounds like eh (short or long). The ch after e or i is a soft hiss from the middle of the tongue (ich, written ç), but after a, o or u it comes from the throat like Arabic kh (Buch, written kh). ß and ss both sound like s, z sounds like ts, and w sounds like v. Finally, every noun has a capital letter. In these lessons the stressed syllable is written in capitals. **Culture:** In German every noun starts with a capital letter, as in Buch and Wasser, which helps you spot nouns when you read. People in Germany like clear pronunciation, but they are usually patient and encouraging with learners, so do not be afraid to try.

You also meet the difference between du for friends and Sie for strangers. **du and Sie:** German has two words for you when you talk to one person: du for people close to you and Sie for formal situations. The formal Sie is always written with a capital letter and takes a plural-style verb form: Wie geht es Ihnen? (formal) and Wie geht es dir? (friendly). The time greetings use Guten because Morgen, Tag and Abend are masculine nouns: Guten Morgen, Guten Tag, Guten Abend. **Culture:** When you enter a small shop say Guten Tag, and when you leave say Tschüss or Auf Wiedersehen. Friends, children and young people use du, while strangers, staff and older people are addressed with Sie. If you are unsure, start with Sie; the other person will usually offer du.

Young people often use first names only, while at work people use a title and surname, such as Herr Weber or Frau Schmidt.

**Explanations to check (English):** At hotels and stations we use polite forms (Konjunktiv II) such as Ich hätte gern and Könnten Sie. For the future we often use the present with a time word, or the verb werden. Here you will book a room and ask about a train. **Politeness with Konjunktiv II and werden:** Ich hätte gern ... is softer than Ich habe ..., and Könnten Sie ...? is softer than Können Sie ...? Both are Konjunktiv II forms. After Könnten the infinitive goes to the end: Könnten Sie mir helfen? For the future: werden (ich werde, du wirst, er wird, wir werden, ihr werdet, sie werden) + infinitive at the end: Ich werde nach Wien fahren. The present with a time word also works: Morgen fahre ich nach Wien. **Culture:** In German hotels breakfast is either included or paid separately, so ask Ist das Frühstück inklusive? For trains you can book in the DB Navigator app or at a machine in the station, and trains can be late, so you may hear Der Zug hat Verspätung.
